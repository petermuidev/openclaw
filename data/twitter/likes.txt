@godofprompt (God of Prompt):
Everyone assumes ChatGPT uses some sophisticated RAG system with vector databases to remember your conversations. Not really. 

After reverse-engineering the entire memory architecture, this developer discovered something way simpler and frankly, smarter. 

ChatGPT uses four distinct layers: ephemeral session metadata, explicit long-term facts, lightweight conversation summaries, and a sliding window of current messages. No embeddings. No similarity searches. No retrieval overhead. Just clean, curated context injection that prioritizes speed over completeness.

The session metadata layer is pure genius from a UX perspective. ChatGPT knows your device type, browser, timezone, subscription level, usage patterns, even your dark mode preference and screen size. This data is injected once per session and never stored permanently. It allows the model to adapt responses to your environment in real-time without cluttering long-term memory. 

Think about it - the model knows you’re on mobile at 2 AM versus desktop during work hours, and can adjust complexity and formatting accordingly. That’s environmental adaptation without the token cost of storing it forever.

The user memory component is where most people focus, but it’s surprisingly minimal. Only 33 explicit facts stored long-term - name, career goals, preferences, projects. These facts are added either when you explicitly say “remember this” or when the model detects something significant and you implicitly confirm through conversation. 

This isn’t passive observation of everything you say. It’s selective, deliberate storage of what actually matters. The key insight: not everything needs to be remembered. Most context is disposable.

Here’s where it gets interesting for anyone building AI products: the recent conversations summary completely bypasses traditional RAG. Instead of embedding every past message and running expensive similarity searches, ChatGPT pre-computes lightweight digests of about 15 recent conversations - just timestamps, titles, and user message snippets. 

No assistant responses included. This creates a loose map of your recent interests without the computational overhead of full-transcript retrieval. It’s trading detailed historical context for speed and token efficiency, and for 95% of use cases, that’s the right trade-off.

The current session uses a standard sliding window approach, but the implementation detail matters. Token count determines the cap, not message count. 

As you approach limits, older messages in the current session roll off, but your memory facts and conversation summaries persist. This means you maintain long-term continuity even as short-term context expires. The architecture acknowledges that not all context has equal value - persistent facts matter more than the middle of a long conversation three days
